**RESPONSE:**

Thank you for the question. It is both a poignant and deeply important one. Let me be clear: when we commit to sending American troops into harm's way, it must be based on sound judgment, global collaboration, and an unwavering dedication to building a sustainable peace—not unilateral decisions or reactive strategies that risk lives unnecessarily.

In the case of Iraq, I believe the question we must ask is not only whether we made mistakes but whether we are learning from them. This is a moment to reflect on the importance of **national security through diplomacy and collaboration.** Wars fought in isolation or based on incomplete information weaken our position globally and do little to address the root causes of instability. Instead of military solutions, we must emphasize partnerships with international allies, robust dialogue, and efforts to address economic and social conditions that often give rise to conflict.

Additionally, we need to adopt **preventative measures and social resilience** as the cornerstone of our foreign policy. Stability in Iraq, and indeed in any region, cannot come solely through force. It requires investment in rebuilding communities, strengthening institutions, and empowering citizens to create sustainable peace from the ground up. That is how we prevent future crises—through patience, foresight, and long-term social investment.

To the broader question of whether lives are being lost in Iraq for a mistake, I say this: no life lost in service to our nation is ever in vain. Every American who serves does so with courage and honor. However, as leaders, we have a profound responsibility to ensure that their sacrifices are not made for short-sighted policies or rushed decisions. This is where **decentralized decision-making and community involvement** become vital. We need to include local voices in Iraq and leverage international expertise to guide our approach, rather than dictating terms unilaterally.

Finally, we must recognize a **moral duty to respect and embrace global diversity.** It is not America's role to impose our values abroad, but rather to work alongside nations to support their unique paths to stability and growth. The mistakes of the past should teach us humility and a commitment to listening—to our allies, to the people on the ground, and to the lessons of history.

The way forward in Iraq—and anywhere we engage as a nation—is through diplomacy, collaboration, and building resilience from within. Let us honor those who have served not by doubling down on conflict, but by doubling down on peace. That is the leadership I will bring as president.
